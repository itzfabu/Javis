# Generational Wealth & Billionaires — Research Notes

*Compiled as a knowledge resource for Jarvis. Drop into `vault/Resources/`.*

## 1. How the Money Actually Gets Made

Recurring patterns across billionaire-wealth research:

- **Own assets, not income.** The consistent theme: hold compounding assets (private companies, real estate, equity stakes) rather than relying on salary. Use debt sparingly, as low-cost leverage against strong cash flow — not to fund lifestyle.
- **Concentrate to build, diversify to keep.** Fortunes are almost always *built* by betting heavily on one business or sector. They're *kept* by spreading risk across asset classes and geographies once the fortune exists. Families that stay wealthy explicitly avoid leaving everything tied to the single business that made them rich.
- **Systems over hustle.** Generational wealth rarely starts with a lump sum — it starts with habits (forced saving, consistent investing) that let compounding run uninterrupted for decades.
- **Decade-scale thinking.** Billionaire investment behavior skews toward long-horizon bets (infrastructure, R&D, emerging sectors) over quarterly optimization.

## 2. Self-Made vs. Inherited — The Actual Numbers

This is genuinely contested depending on methodology:

- **Forbes' framework:** 67% of the 2025 World's Billionaires List (up from 66% in 2024) are self-made; the ~990 heirs on the list hold a combined $5.3T (33% of total). Huge country variance — China and Russia ~97% self-made; Germany only ~25% self-made (rest are heirs). Gender gap: only 113 self-made women globally; nearly three-quarters of female billionaires inherited their fortunes. ([Forbes 2025 List](https://www.forbes.com/sites/chasewithorn/2025/04/01/forbes-39th-annual-worlds-billionaires-list-more-than-3000-worth-16-trillion/))
- **Forbes' actual scoring is a 1–10 scale**, not a binary — staff-assigned, qualitative, not algorithmic (worth noting as a limitation of an otherwise transparent, dated source):

  | Score | Definition |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Inherited entire fortune, not actively growing it |
  | 2–4 | Inherited fortune, growing it to varying degrees |
  | 5 | Inherited a small/medium business, built it into a 10-figure fortune |
  | 6 | Hired executive who didn't found the business |
  | 7 | Self-made but had a wealthy family head start |
  | 8 | Self-made, middle/upper-middle-class background (most common score) |
  | 9 | Self-made, working-class origins |
  | 10 | Self-made, grew up poor / overcame significant adversity |

- **The trend is shifting toward inheritance — but track the report year, they don't all agree.** UBS's *Billionaire Ambitions Report* is published annually and the self-made/heir balance has swung year to year:
  - **2023 report** (data to Apr 2023): 84 self-made ($140.7B) vs. 53 heirs ($150.8B) — the first time in the report's 9-year history that new heirs' combined wealth exceeded new self-made wealth. *(This is the source of the "$150.8B/53 heirs" stat — it's from 2023, not 2025; a mislabel corrected here.)*
  - **2024 report**: 268 new billionaires, ~60% entrepreneurs (~161 self-made vs. ~107 heirs) — self-made regained the lead.
  - **2025 report**, "The Rise of a New Generation": 196 self-made vs. 91 heirs (self-made still leads on headcount), but the 91 heirs inherited a record $297.8B (+36% YoY). Multi-generational billionaires now total 860 people / $4.7T (up from 805/$4.2T in 2024): 2nd-gen wealth +4.6%, 3rd-gen +12.3%, 4th-gen+ +10%. Billionaires are projected to transfer ~$6.9T by 2040 (~$5.9T to children). Inheritance is now the single most common path for *women* becoming billionaires (27 of 43 new women billionaires in 2025 were heirs vs. 16 self-made). ([UBS 2025](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/media/display-page-ndp/en-20251204-billionaire-ambitions-report-2025.html))
- **UBS Global Family Office Report 2025** (separate report, survey of family offices rather than a billionaire census): only 53% of family offices have a succession plan for family members; only 35% have one for the family office as an institution; only 27% have any organized process to prepare the next generation; only 26% consult the next generation from the outset. Greatest cited succession challenge: tax-efficient transfer (64%), preparing heirs to handle wealth responsibly (43%). Context: ~$83T in assets is projected to change hands over the next two-plus decades. ([UBS Global Family Office Report 2025](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/media/display-page-ndp/en-20250521-global-family-office-report-2025.html))
- **Oxfam's contrasting framing:** *"Takers Not Makers: The Unjust Poverty and Unearned Wealth of Colonialism"* (Oxfam International, Jan 20, 2025, published for WEF Davos) attributes 60% of billionaire wealth to inheritance (36%), monopoly power (18%), or cronyism/corruption (6%) — a much less flattering read than Forbes' "self-made" scoring, and a genuinely different framework rather than just a stricter version of Forbes' scale: Forbes scores *individuals* on effort/background, Oxfam buckets wealth by structural *source type*. Also per Oxfam: for the first time (2024 data), more new billionaires got rich through inheritance than entrepreneurship by their classification; all billionaires under 30 inherited their wealth. ([Oxfam](https://www.oxfam.org/en/research/takers-not-makers-unjust-poverty-and-unearned-wealth-colonialism))
  - **How the 36/18/6% is actually calculated (methodology traced):** the 2025 note defers to a decade-old classification framework — Didier Jacobs, *"Extreme Wealth Is Not Merited"* (Oxfam, 2015) — rather than a fresh 2025 method. Base data is Forbes' Real-Time Billionaire List. **Inheritance:** 100% of an heir's *current* net worth is counted as "inherited," even if the fortune has grown many times over since it was received — no attempt to isolate the original sum from the heir's own subsequent value creation. **Monopoly power:** entire industries are pre-labeled "monopoly-prone" (tech/network-effects, finance, healthcare, legal services, even fashion/retail and sports in the 2025 note), and *all* self-made wealth in a labeled industry is then treated as monopoly-derived, with no firm-by-firm test of whether that specific company actually holds market power. **Cronyism:** flagged by whether a billionaire operates in a country ranked corruption-prone (external indices) and/or an industry deemed structurally state-dependent (oil, gas, mining) — again categorical, not case-specific. ([Oxfam Methodology Note, Jan 2025](https://www.oxfamfrance.org/app/uploads/2025/01/Oxfam-Davos-2025-Methodology-Note.pdf); [Jacobs 2015 original](https://www-cdn.oxfam.org/s3fs-public/file_attachments/dp-extreme-wealth-is-not-merited-241115-en.pdf))
  - **The critique (from free-market economists, not neutral academics — flagged as one-sided below):** Cato Institute calls the industry-wide labeling "profound analytical bankruptcy" — theoretical possibility of market failure in a sector is treated as proof that *all* billionaire wealth in it is ill-gained. IREF Europe flags that net-wealth-only framing ignores income, pensions, and future earning power. AEI's James Pethokoukis argues the report omits that extreme poverty has fallen by hundreds of millions globally over the same period, and blends within-country and between-country inequality trends that shouldn't be combined. ([Cato/Human Progress](https://humanprogress.org/oxfam-is-entitled-to-its-own-opinions-but-not-its-own-facts/); [IREF Europe](https://en.irefeurope.org/publications/online-articles/article/problems-with-oxfam-s-inequality-report/); [AEI](https://www.aei.org/economics/why-the-much-hyped-oxfam-study-on-global-inequality-is-misleading/))
  - **Open gap, stated plainly:** every critique found is from a market-liberal-aligned think tank (Cato, AEI, IREF). No neutral or left-of-center academic methodological critique (as opposed to a critique of Oxfam's political framing) was located. Oxfam has published its own rebuttal ("What Critics Get Wrong About Inequality & Oxfam") which wasn't read in full here. Treat the methodology critique above as real and specific, but sourced from one side of the debate.
- **Takeaway:** "self-made" is doing a lot of definitional work, and even within one source (UBS), the year-to-year headline can flip. Always cite report + year, not just the organization.

## 3. Why It Disappears Just as Fast — The "Shirtsleeves" Pattern

The most-cited finding in wealth-preservation research: **"shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations"** (American) / "wealth doesn't last three generations" (Chinese proverb) — the same pattern observed across cultures.

- **The Williams Group** (Roy O. Williams & Vic Preisser, *Preparing Heirs*, 2003): claims a "20-year study of 2,500 families" plus a further 750-family project with Miami University's Farmer School of Business (~3,250 total, roughly the "3,200+" figure). 70% of families lose control of wealth/assets by end of 2nd generation; 90% by end of 3rd. Cause breakdown per the book: ~60% breakdown of family trust/communication, ~25% inadequate heir preparation, remainder legal/tax/other. **Credibility caveat: no public methodology paper, no peer review, no independent replication exists — this is proprietary consulting-firm research summarized in a trade book, from a firm that also sells the advisory services that "fix" the problem.** Treat the 70/90% figures as a widely-repeated industry stat, not a rigorous study.
- **Illinois manufacturing study** (single 1980s study, Illinois firms, 1924–1984): ~13% of family businesses were still *operating* through a third 60-year generational block. Caveats: this measures company survival as an operating entity, not family wealth; it doesn't distinguish a business *failing* from an owner simply *selling* it (both count as "not surviving"); and it has no baseline comparison — public companies of that era averaged only ~15 years of existence, so 13%-through-60-years may not even be a bad outcome relatively speaking.
- **Mechanism isn't laziness — it's structural.** The research points to: lack of open family communication about money, next generation not being taught financial literacy early, lifestyle creep away from the frugality that built the fortune, and heirs having no real involvement in decisions before inheriting.
- **What beats the pattern:** continuous financial education (not just elite schooling), regular family meetings about money and values, giving the next generation real influence over the family business before they inherit it outright, and formal structures — trusts, family offices, estate plans revisited on a schedule rather than set-and-forget.
- **The "curse" framing itself is contested.** Dennis Jaffe ([Forbes, 2019](https://www.forbes.com/sites/dennisjaffe/2019/01/28/the-shirtsleeves-to-shirtsleeves-curse-how-family-wealth-can-survive-it/)) argues heirs internalize guilt/anxiety from being told they're statistically doomed to fail, are then treated as unable to handle responsibility, and underperform as a result — a self-fulfilling prophecy. Daniel Trimarchi (KPMG Canada, via [Canadian Family Offices](https://canadianfamilyoffices.com/fundamentals/time-to-set-fire-to-shirtsleeves-adage-about-family-wealth-advisors-say/)) goes further: the adage has been commercially useful to the family-wealth advisory industry itself — "a family convinced that seven out of ten families like them lose everything becomes a buyer before an advisor says a word about services." Both are practitioner/opinion critiques, not counter-studies — credible as industry pushback, not as empirical refutation.

## 4. Operating Principles — How to Treat Business & Work

*This section exists to be used, not just read: a set of heuristics Jarvis should actually apply when Fabio is making business/money decisions, not just a summary of what rich people did. Sourced primarily from Buffett/Munger (Berkshire shareholder letters, speeches) and Bezos (Amazon shareholder letters), cross-checked against family-business governance research.*

- **Capital allocation is mostly saying no.** Buffett: "The trick is, when there is nothing to do, do nothing." Munger has said his lifetime returns trace to roughly four decisions; Buffett to about a dozen. The operating rule: set a real hurdle (return threshold, strategic fit), reject nearly everything against it, and commit heavily — not diversify thin — on the rare opportunity that clears it. **Apply this to Fabio's projects:** when evaluating a new idea (streamer-hud, jarvis-as-a-service, etc.), the default answer should be "pass" unless it clears a real bar, not "sounds interesting, let's explore." ([Berkshire letters](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf); [Forbes, Apr 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/robertdaugherty/2026/04/25/ignore-the-noise-how-a-handful-of-bets-drives-outsized-returns/))
- **Opportunity cost as the screening filter, not case-by-case analysis.** Munger: "Opportunity cost is a huge filter in life... that's the way we filter out buying opportunities." Practically: compare a new commitment against the best thing already in motion (time, money, focus), not in isolation — most things lose that comparison immediately, which is the point. ([GuruFocus compilation](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/2080637/the-unseen-costs-of-missed-opportunities-investing-wisdom-from-buffett-munger-and-klarman))
- **Delegate execution completely; keep capital allocation centralized.** Berkshire's model — Munger called it "delegation just short of abdication": subsidiary CEOs get full operational autonomy (no mandated plans, no approval chains for normal decisions), but *excess cash* still gets pulled to the center for reallocation. The two are deliberately separate — trust on execution is not the same as trust on where money goes next. ([Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/berkshire-hathaways-culture-of-trust/); [Stanford GSB case study](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/case-studies/management-berkshire-hathaway))
- **Reversible vs. irreversible decisions get different speeds.** Bezos's "one-way door / two-way door" framework: irreversible calls deserve slow, heavy scrutiny; the large majority of decisions are reversible and should be made fast, by a small group or one person, without the process weight appropriate to a one-way door. Misapplying heavy process to a reversible decision is itself a cost (velocity lost for no safety gained). ([Bezos shareholder letters](https://amazonbound.medium.com/bezoss-letters-to-amazon-s-shareholders-e220096787bb))
- **"Day 1" discipline against internal drift.** Bezos, restated annually: "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline." Defense: stay obsessed with the actual customer/user rather than internal proxies (competitors, process metrics, the plan itself), resist the pull toward decision-by-committee, and adopt useful external trends quickly instead of resisting them. ([Bezos 1997/annual letters](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF))
- **Survival outranks upside — always.** Munger/Buffett: "Smart men go broke three ways: liquor, ladies, and leverage" — leverage is the one that actually matters. Buffett, 2010 letter: "History tells us that leverage all too often produces zeros, even when it is employed by very smart people." The operating rule isn't "avoid debt," it's *sequence*: never take a risk where the downside is ruin, no matter how good the upside looks — check this before checking expected return, not after. ([CNBC, 2018](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/02/26/buffett-says-out-of-the-three-ways-to-go-broke-liquor-ladies-and-leverage-leverage-is-the-worst.html))
- **Concentrate to build, diversify to keep — the trigger is "something worth protecting exists," not a calendar date.** No primary source gives a clean numeric threshold for when to shift (flagged as a real gap in the sourcing — don't invent a specific percentage or net-worth figure if this comes up in advice). The directional rule that *is* well-supported: while a venture is still being built, concentration is close to unavoidable — spreading thin early just slows the compounding that makes the bet worth taking. Diversification becomes the priority only once the position is large enough that a single-point failure would be catastrophic rather than merely costly.
- **Bringing someone new into real responsibility is a staged process, not a single handoff.** Family-business governance research documents concrete, gradual mechanisms rather than "just give them influence": rotation through real functions, mentorship from experienced non-family staff, observing decisions before having a vote, a real (if limited) seat before full authority, and a written document (family constitution / equivalent) spelling out the rules of involvement rather than leaving it implicit. **Apply this pattern generally** to onboarding anyone (a collaborator, a hire, a delegated agent) into a domain they don't yet have full context on — staged visibility and limited authority first, full authority once demonstrated, not a binary switch. ([Family Business Magazine](https://www.familybusinessmagazine.com/governance/nurturing-an-engaged-next-generation-through-onboarding/); [IMD Global Family Business Center](https://www.imd.org/centers/gfbc/imd-family-business-center/research-insights/))

## 6. Methodology Note

Forbes (annual, interview + document-based, transparent but subjective 1–10 staff scoring), Bloomberg Billionaires Index (daily, market-data-based), Oxfam (activist framing, buckets wealth by structural source type — inheritance/monopoly/cronyism — rather than scoring individuals), and UBS (two distinct reports: the annual *Billionaire Ambitions Report*, a billionaire census, vs. the *Global Family Office Report*, a survey of family offices) all measure overlapping things differently. When citing a stat, note which source **and which year** it came from — UBS's own self-made/heir balance has flipped between its 2023, 2024, and 2025 editions, and the self-made/inherited split can shift by 10–40 points depending on which organization's framework you use.

**Primary-source status by claim (as of this pass):**
- Forbes self-made %, scoring scale, UBS report stats, Oxfam headline % and calculation logic — traced to named, dated primary reports and their underlying methodology (§2).
- Williams Group 70/90% and Illinois 13% figures — traced to their original trade-book/study source, but neither has a public methodology paper or independent replication; treat as industry-repeated claims, not verified rigorous research.
- Oxfam's 36/18/6% split — calculation method traced to Jacobs (2015), reused unchanged in the 2025 report; the categorical (industry-wide, not firm-specific) labeling logic is confirmed, but critiques of it found so far are one-sided (free-market think tanks only) — no neutral/independent methodological review located yet.
- Operating Principles (§4) — sourced to primary shareholder letters/speeches (Buffett, Munger, Bezos) plus family-business governance research (Family Business Magazine, IMD); a few individual quotes came via consistent secondary compilation rather than a re-verified original PDF (noted inline where relevant), and the "concentrate → diversify" trigger point has no rigorous numeric source — treated as directional, not a documented rule.
